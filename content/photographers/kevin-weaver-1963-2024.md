Kevin Michael Weaver was born on 7 December 1963. After his mother died of cancer when he was seventeen, he described the loss as the turning point of his life, one that led him through a turbulent youth before he studied art at a Brighton polytechnic, a path that eventually steered him toward photojournalism.
Weaver began his career documenting the political upheavals that swept Eastern Europe at the end of the Cold War, covering the revolutions in East Germany, Czechoslovakia and Romania as the old communist order collapsed 1. It was while freelancing for The Guardian in the early 1990s, however, that he found the story that would define his career: the war in Bosnia, which he later said became "almost an obsession" for him. In June 1992, at the age of twenty-eight, Weaver arrived alone in besieged Sarajevo, with no flak jacket, no fixer and no support network. On 29 June 1992, he was seriously wounded by sniper fire while driving the exposed airport road alongside French journalist Jean Hatzfeld of Libération. A bullet struck Weaver's leg and lodged a centimetre from his spine; doctors removed it without anaesthetic or X-rays in Bosnia's battered wartime hospitals. He became the first journalist medevacked by the UN out of Sarajevo. He returned to Bosnia just six months later, as committed as ever and went on to document the siege of Sarajevo and its aftermath for over a decade, producing some of the defining images of the Siege. He later attended war crimes trials at The Hague, driven, he said, by a need to understand "how this war was allowed to happen" after years of simply reacting to bullets and hunger on the ground. In 1994, Weaver travelled to Rwanda to cover the genocide, bearing witness to some of the worst atrocities of the decade. The toll of more than a decade in conflict zones eventually caught up with him: Weaver was diagnosed with PTSD in 2003. Feeling burnt out, he stepped back from war reporting, working for a time as a paparazzi photographer and later as a teacher. He eventually settled in Cumbria, where he found a second creative chapter as a painter, singer-songwriter and musician. Early in his painting career his work drew heavily on his war memories, though he later said he had "got it all out" and moved on, preferring not to keep reliving the past. He exhibited his artwork at the Lowes Court Gallery in Egremont and performed at open-mic nights at the Florence Mine. He died on 16 September 2024, aged 60, survived by Linda and his brother, Gary.
